### Pieces of an Abstract:

Abstracts are like the "elevator pitch" of your idea to a conference and to attendees. They should be concise enough to be readable, but detailed enough to explain what value the session holds. You don't have to prepare answers for all possible questions within this written statement, but you should be clear about your learning objectives for the attendees.

***
### Example "elevator pitch":

*Call to adventure:*\
An Apple a Day is Great!

*Conflict:*\
Sometimes when a person doesn't eat fruit, they can become malnourished. Eating an apple can help give you some of the nutrition that you need on a daily basis.

*Benefits:*\
Nutritional value, many varieties for wide range of tastes, relatively low cost comparatively.

*"Elevator pitch":*\
An apple a day keeps the doctor away - or at least that's how the saying goes. I believe this is true, because my experience has been positive when I eat apples. I have seen...(*insert examples of benefits here*) that supports this claim. If you want to know more about how this fruit can help keep you healthy, come and join my session!

### Feedback structures:
** Note - these feedback structures are commonly used in conflict situations, or where behavior correction is needed. You may find that the methods aren't quite perfect for every use case, but if you're stuck on what to say - this is a good place to start!

**COIN**
- *Context*: Explain when and where the observed behavior occurred.
- *Observation*: State factual observations, not interpretations.
- *Impact*: Share the effect of the behavior.
- *Next Steps*: Discuss actions or changes needed for the future.

For our purposes, **COIN** would be useful for suggesting alternate viewpoints that may not have been considered by the author when writing their abstract. Be careful not to be dismissive of the stance of the author (even if you disagree with their perspective), but offer strategies that can help them strengthen their arguments.


**SBI** 
- *Situation*: Describe the specific situation where the behavior occurred.
- *Behavior*: Detail the actual, observable behavior without interpretation or judgment.
- *Impact*: Explain the impact of the behavior and how it affected you, the team, or the project.

When reviewing each others' abstracts, **SBI** can be useful to help you provide constructive praise to the author about their ideas. Describe the portion that you enjoyed/related to/clearly understood. Tell the author why you feel this was a positive addition to their abstract. Explain how this portion inspired additional questions or interest in their topic.

**STAR**
- *Situation*: Set the context for the story.
- *Task*: Describe the task and the challenge involved.
- *Action*: Explain the actions taken.
- *Result*: Share the outcomes of those actions.

If you find yourself disagreeing with the author on a fundamental portion of their abstract, you can utilize the **STAR** method to help them consider how their presentation materials will cover the topic. Be specific in the areas that you have experience in, to set the context for what you disagree with. Maybe you struggled with this in the past, or made mistakes that the author can learn about from you (we're all human, after all). Tell the author how you found this information, or provide sources to research on the subject for further exploration. Be clear about how this viewpoint has positively impacted your experience since that time.
